A useful distinction

“The first paycheck” is an interpretation used for the title. A surviving record of beer rations does not establish the very first wage in human history.

The record

What we can actually say

The opening minute is grounded in British Museum object 1989,0130.4, a Late Uruk clay tablet. The institution’s own entry is available through Google Arts & Culture. [1]

The date
The museum dates this particular object to approximately 3100–3000 BC, in southern Iraq, Mesopotamia. That dating belongs to this object; it is not a claim about the first wage. [1]
The record
The museum interprets the text as beer allocated to workers as part of daily rations. “Rations,” “payment” and a modern cash paycheck are not interchangeable categories. [1]
The signs
The description identifies a vessel-shaped beer sign with waves inside, appearing three times. A bowl directed toward a head denotes eating and helps identify the record as concerning rations. Three signs do not demonstrate three drinks consumed. [1]

“some of the earliest writing”

British Museum, description of object 1989,0130.4. [1]

This short quotation describes the writing, not a universally established first paycheck.

The fine print

What the comedy does not claim

  • The tablet does not establish humanity’s first wage, first beer or first employer.
  • No universal daily quantity in litres is asserted for every Mesopotamian worker.
  • Office props, comic reactions and modern-payday comparisons are fictional metaphors, not ancient testimony.
  • Drawn clay props and schematic signs are illustrations, not photographs or exact transcriptions of museum artifacts.
  • A later messenger record is not evidence that every earlier worker received the same arrangement.
